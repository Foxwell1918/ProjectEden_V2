## Center: The Heart of Storytelling — Plot, Promise, Progress, and Payoff

### **Story Components and Conflict**

Sanderson begins by framing stories as constructs built from **three fundamental elements**:

- **Plot**
- **Setting**
- **Character**

The intersection of these elements produces **conflict**, which is the *core* of any story. Conflict arises when characters face obstacles within their environment or circumstances, driving the narrative forward. Sanderson underscores that **all three components should serve to create or facilitate conflict**, as stories fundamentally revolve around characters striving to overcome challenges.

### **Understanding Plot**

Sanderson categorizes **plot** into two types:

| **Type** | **Description** | **Example** |
|------------|-------------------|--------------|
| **Big P Plot** | The *main* story premise, the *what* the book is about in a nutshell | *Destroying the One Ring in *The Lord of the Rings*.* |
| **Little p Plot** | The *chapter-by-chapter* problems that keep readers engaged | *Getting past orcs in *The Lord of the Rings*.* |

He emphasizes that **plot's primary purpose** is to **facilitate conflict** and **provide structure**. It guides the reader through the story, giving a sense of progression and purpose. Sanderson notes that **plot is the most challenging element to innovate upon** because of audience expectations and the need for satisfying resolution.

### **Storytelling Archetypes and Frameworks**

While next week’s lectures will delve into **plot frameworks** like the Hero’s Journey, Sanderson clarifies that **most stories follow a core structure**: **promise**, **progress**, and **payoff**. These elements underpin many plot frameworks, whether archetypal or subverted.

### **Promise, Progress, and Payoff**

- **Promise**: The *initial expectation* set by the story, often through the opening scene, tone, or early chapters.
- **Progress**: The *movement* toward fulfilling that promise, measured by character actions, obstacles overcome, or information revealed.
- **Payoff**: The *resolution* that fulfills the promise, ideally surprising yet inevitable, satisfying the reader’s expectations.

Sanderson stresses that **these three elements** are **interdependent**. A failure in one often results in a story feeling unsatisfying or disjointed.

## Intro: The Role of Promises in Storytelling

### **Promises as a Foundation**

Sanderson advocates that **promises** are **the easiest and most vital** aspect to manage early in a story. They set **reader expectations** and **guide the narrative’s emotional trajectory**. Promises can be **explicit** (e.g., a character’s goal) or **implicit** (e.g., tone, setting).

**Types of Promises** include:

- **Plot Promises**: The main goal or conflict (e.g., destroy the ring).
- **Tone Promises**: The *feel* of the story (e.g., humor, tragedy).
- **Character Promises**: Character arcs or flaws (e.g., a hero’s journey of growth).

### **Signposting and Early Promises**

Sanderson emphasizes **signposting**—the subtle cues that hint at what’s to come. Effective signposting **builds anticipation** and **prepares readers** for the story’s twists and turns. For example:

- **Prologues** that set tone or hint at conflict.
- **Chapter openings** that introduce mini-promises.
- **Names and descriptions** that evoke mood.

He illustrates this with examples like **George R.R. Martin’s *Wheel of Time***, which begins with a tone-setting prologue hinting at chaos and power.

### **Breaking the Rules**

Sanderson notes that **storytelling rules are flexible**. Writers like Shakespeare with *Hamlet* or movies like *Pulp Fiction* intentionally **skip or subvert traditional acts**. The key is **intentionality**—knowing why you deviate and ensuring it serves the story.

## Center: Deep Dive into Promises and Their Management

### **Building Promises**

Sanderson advises writers to **craft promises that align with their story’s tone and structure**. For example:

- **Tone Promise**: If your story is humorous, early scenes should reflect that (e.g., naming characters whimsically).
- **Plot Promise**: The main goal should be clear early on, such as *destroying the ring* in *Lord of the Rings*.
- **Character Promise**: Character flaws or arcs should be hinted at, like *Gandalf’s wisdom* or *Frodo’s burden*.

He discusses **series promises**—how to maintain continuity across multiple books:

- **Series Promises**: A larger goal or theme that spans multiple installments (e.g., *The Wheel of Time* promises the Dragon Reborn’s ultimate confrontation).
- **Individual Book Promises**: Smaller, contained goals that contribute to the larger promise.

### **Promises in Series and Sequels**

Sanderson highlights that **series promises** can be **explicit** (e.g., *a seven-book series about seven sisters*) or **implicit** (e.g., *a character’s ongoing arc*). The **packaging** (titles, blurbs) also **sets expectations**.

### **Promises and Pacing**

He emphasizes **balancing promises with pacing**—not overloading the reader with too many promises at once, but **layering promises** to **maintain interest**. For example, **initial promises** might be **tone and character**, while **later promises** involve **plot revelations**.

## Outro: The Power of Progress and Payoff

### **Measuring Progress**

Sanderson stresses that **writers control progress**—the *how* and *when* of story advancement. **Progress** can be **external** (e.g., characters reaching a destination) or **internal** (e.g., character growth). Effective storytelling **signposts progress** through:

- **Chapter endings** that hint at upcoming developments.
- **Obstacles** that escalate tension.
- **Information reveals** that move the plot forward.

He warns against **stagnation**—when the story **fails to deliver on promises**, leading to reader boredom or frustration.

### **Pacing and Signposting**

The **pace** should **match the story’s tone and structure**. For fast-paced thrillers, **short chapters with hooks** work well; for slower, character-driven stories, **breathers and reflection** are necessary. Sanderson advises **varying pacing** to **maintain reader engagement**.

### **Handling Travel and Time Jumps**

To **speed up travel scenes**, Sanderson recommends **early promises** of **time jumps** (e.g., “and then they arrived”). **Chapter breaks** or **signaling phrases** like “three weeks later” help **manage pacing** without confusing readers.

### **Twists and Surprises**

**Effective twists** rely on **signposting**—**foreshadowing** and **misdirection**. Sanderson compares twists to **gifts**—**surprising yet inevitable**. He illustrates this with examples like ***While You Were Sleeping***, where **escalation** (e.g., a lie revealed) **creates emotional payoff**.

He emphasizes **escalation**—**building obstacles**—and **red herrings**—**misleading clues**—to **maximize surprise**. The **ultimate goal** is **satisfying resolution**, where **payoff** feels **both surprising and earned**.

## Final Thoughts and Practical Advice

- **Understand your promises**: They set expectations and guide your story.
- **Signpost effectively**: Use early cues to prepare readers for twists and resolutions.
- **Control progress**: Decide how fast or slow your story moves, matching pacing to genre and tone.
- **Layer multiple promises**: Especially in series, to keep readers engaged across installments.
- **Be willing to deviate**: Rules are flexible; intentional deviations can create memorable stories.
- **Use escalation and red herrings**: To craft compelling twists and maintain suspense.
- **Balance worldbuilding, character, and plot**: While all are important, **great plot and characters** often outweigh **worldbuilding** in storytelling impact.
