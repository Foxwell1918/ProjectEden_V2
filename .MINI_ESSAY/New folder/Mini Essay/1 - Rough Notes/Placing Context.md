**2. Poetry as a Conversation**

**3. Types of Contexts in Poetry**
- **Biographical Context:** The poet’s life influences the poem.

- **Universal Context:** Themes and ideas that resonate beyond specific personal or historical moments.

# Descriptive Summary: The Power of Context in Close Reading of Poetry

## Introduction

Welcome to the first lecture on **closer reading**, where we explore the foundational concept of **placing in context**. This session emphasizes that understanding poetry extends beyond mere external research; it involves perceiving poems as **conversations**—dynamic exchanges with other works, poets, traditions, and cultural references. Recognizing these dialogues enriches our interpretation, revealing layers of meaning that transcend individual words. Through examples spanning classical, Renaissance, Victorian, and modern poetry, we will see how context shapes our comprehension and appreciation of poetic art.

---

## Placing in Context: Beyond Outside Material

Traditionally, **placing in context** involves researching historical, biographical, or cultural background. While sometimes necessary, this approach can be **laborious and detached** from the poem's essence. Instead, the lecture advocates for viewing context as a **conversation**—a **dialogue** between poems, poets, and traditions.

### Poetry as a Conversation

- **Poets live in dialogue with predecessors and contemporaries.**
- **Poetry evolves through reinterpretation and reinvention of earlier modes.**
- **Rich figurative language, imagery, and themes are often responses or references to earlier works.**

**Example:** John Keats's engagement with Edmund Spencer's *The Fairy Queen* illustrates how Keats absorbed Spencer's inventive epithets, such as *sea shouldering whales*, and incorporated them into his own poetry, creating a **continuity of poetic tradition**.

---

## Keats and Spencer: A Case Study

Keats's admiration for Spencer's **epithets**—short descriptive phrases—demonstrates how poets **enter into a conversation** with previous poets. Spencer's inventive epithets like:

| **Epithets** | **Description** |
|--------------|-----------------|
| *a troop of light-footed deities* | Vivid imagery of divine beings |
| *blood red billows* | Descriptive of the sea |
| *froth foamy steed* | A lively metaphor for a horse |
| *bright dew burning blade* | Descriptive of a sword |

Keats's own poetry, such as his sonnet *When I have fears that I may cease to be*, contains epithets like:

- *Fair creature of an hour*
- *High pilot books in charactery*
- *Magic hand of chance*
- *Unreflecting love*

These reflect Spencer's influence, illustrating how **poetry is a dialogue** across time, with each poet building upon and transforming earlier imagery and language.

## The Broader Significance of Context

### Literature as a Community

George McDonald emphasized that **all songs and poems** are part of a **living tradition**. No poem exists in isolation; each is **interconnected** within a **community of voices**. Recognizing this **collective context** enhances our understanding and appreciation.

### Types of Conversations in Poetry

Poetry can **converse** through various **contexts**:

| **Type of Context** | **Description** | **Example** |
|---------------------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Biographical** | Poems related to the poet's life or historical figures | Wyatt's *Whoso List to Hunt* |
| **Historical** | Poems responding to or reflecting specific events | Shakespeare's plays defying classical unities |
| **Illusions** | References to biblical or classical texts | Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* referencing Diana and Cupid |
| **Collective/Collection** | Poems within larger collections influencing each other | Shakespeare's sonnets, Herbert's *The Temple* |

## Biographical and Historical Contexts

### Wyatt's *Whoso List to Hunt*

- **Biographical background:** Wyatt's imprisonment and possible romantic connection to Anne Boleyn add layers to the poem.
- **Poem summary:** The speaker laments the futility of pursuing a deer (possibly representing Boleyn or a woman of high status), emphasizing **vain effort** with metaphors like *seeking to hold the wind* and *around her neck Noli me tangeri* (Latin for "touch me not").
- **Universal theme:** The pursuit of fleeting love or ideals that are ultimately unattainable.

### Universal vs. Particular

- **Particular:** Specific to Wyatt's life and times.
- **Universal:** The human experience of chasing after **ephemeral desires**—romantic or aspirational—that resonate across ages.

---

## Illusions: Enriching Meaning

### Classical Illusions

- Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet* references **Diana** and **Cupid**.
- **Diana:** The goddess of the hunt, associated with virginity and chastity.
- **Illusion's role:** Adds **depth** and **shared cultural knowledge**, making the text **more accessible** to contemporary audiences.

### Biblical Illusions

- Poe's *The Raven* references **Gilead**—a biblical land of healing.
- **"Is there balm in Gilead?"**: A plea for **healing** and **hope**.
- **Function:** Connects the poem to **religious and cultural traditions**, deepening its emotional impact.

---

## The Power of Illusions

- **Enrich associations:** Illusions condense complex ideas into **compact references**.
- **Bridge or obstacle:** While some see illusions as **obscure**, they are **shared cultural codes** that **bridge** poet and reader.
- **Example:** Shakespeare's classical illusions are **accessible** because of common knowledge, not **show-offs**.

---

## Collective Context in Collections

Poems within collections often **interact** through their **placement**:

- **Example:** George Herbert's *The Altar* follows *The Church Porch*, mirroring the **layout of a church**.
- **Implication:** The **order** of poems influences their **meaning** and **interpretation**.

**Key Point:** When reading collections, **consider the sequence** to fully grasp the **collective conversation**.

## Literary History and Innovation

### Shakespeare's Break with Tradition

- **The Unities:** Classical rule of **one action**, **one day**, **one location**.
- **Shakespeare's approach:** Plays spanning **years** and **multiple locations**—a **radical departure**.
- **Impact:** His innovations **expanded the possibilities** of drama, influencing future generations.

### Wordsworth's Daffodils

- **Context:** Writing about simple rural scenes in a **new, elevated diction**.
- **Revolution:** Made **ordinary nature** a subject worthy of **poetry**, challenging previous notions of poetic grandeur.

---

## Love Poetry: Inventiveness and Tradition

### The Blazon Tradition

- **Definition:** Cataloging a woman's **physical features**.
- **Example:** Spencer's *The Faerie Queene* describes virtues through physical attributes.
- **Evolution:** Spencer **adds** the **virtue** of her **mind**, transforming the **blazon** into a **celebration of inner qualities**.

### Shakespeare's Sonnet 130

- **Rejection of hyperbole:** Praises the mistress **without false comparisons**.
- **Lines:** *My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun...*
- **Significance:** A **counter-tradition**—genuine love over exaggerated praise—**speaking back** to earlier poetic conventions.

---

## Cavalier Love Poetry and Carpe Diem

### Robert Herrick's *To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time*

- **Theme:** Urgency of seizing youth and beauty.
- **Imagery:** *Gather ye rosebuds while ye may*—flowers symbolize fleeting life.
- **Message:** **Live fully** before time runs out.

### Edmund Waller's *Go, Lovely Rose*

- **Theme:** Beauty's fragility.
- **Message:** Beauty must be **praised and enjoyed** before it fades.

### Andrew Marvell's *To His Coy Mistress*

- **Three movements:**
  1. **Hypothetical:** If time were endless, love could be leisurely.
  2. **Realization:** Time is **hurrying**—death is inevitable.
  3. **Application:** **Act now**—love must **devour** time before it devours us.
- **Poetic technique:** Rapid meter and energetic language **build to a passionate conclusion**.
- **Significance:** A **masterpiece** of **Carpe Diem** poetry, blending **argument** and **rebellion** against mortality.

---

## Keats: A Life in Conversation

- **Keats's tragic brevity:** Died of tuberculosis at age 25.
- **Final words:** *"Here lies one whose name was writ in water."*
- **Meaning:** His life and poetry were **interwoven**—a **dialogue** with poetry and mortality.
- **Legacy:** His **poems** continue the **conversation** across centuries, embodying the **community of poets**.

## Conclusion

**Placing in context** is not merely an academic exercise but an **invitation** to **participate** in the **ongoing conversation** of poetry. Recognizing how poems **speak to each other**—through **biography**, **history**, **illusions**, and **collections**—allows us to **hear** the **voices of the past** and **appreciate** the **innovations** of each poet. As we deepen our **close reading skills**, we unlock the **layers of meaning** embedded in poetic dialogue, enriching our understanding and enjoyment of this timeless art form.
