## What is Close Reading and why is it Important?

It is a method of reading Poetry.
A close reading examines many things about the poem.

## The Five Steps of Close Reading.

In his five steps to Close Reading, there are the acronym P.O.E.M.S.

**P** : Place in Contexts
**O** : Observe Words and Language
**E** : Experience Impressions
**M** : Mark Meter
**S** : Survey Structure

Some of these steps are more important than another.

## 1. Placing in Context

Understanding a poem begins with situating it within its **biographical, historical, political, religious, and literary contexts**. This step involves asking:

- *Who is the poet?* (biography, speaker vs. poet)
- *When and where was it written?* (historical background)
- *What are the political or religious influences?*
- *How does the poem relate to literary traditions or reactions?*

**Why is this important?** Context enriches interpretation, revealing allusions, themes, and nuances that might otherwise be missed. For example, recognizing a religious allusion in George Herbert’s poetry deepens appreciation of its moral and spiritual layers.

How does a poem react against tradition? 

## 2. Observing Words and Language

This step is the core of close reading, focusing on **diction** (word choice) and **devices** (figures of speech, schemes). Key considerations include:

| Aspect | Questions to Ask |
|---------|------------------|
| **Word Roots** | Latin, Greek origins? |
| **Sound & Rhythm** | What sounds do words evoke? |
| **Imagery & Associations** | What images or ideas do words conjure? |
| **Figurative Language** | Metaphors, similes, allegories? |
| **Placement & Structure** | How are words arranged schematically? |

**Purpose:** Analyzing words reveals how language shapes meaning, tone, and emotional impact. For instance, the choice of monosyllabic words might create a stark, direct tone, while multisyllabic words could evoke complexity or grandeur.

## 3. Experiencing Impressions

Rooted in Samuel Johnson’s concept, **Impressions** refer to the **mental and emotional effects** a poem leaves. This involves:

- **Sensory Impressions:** How does the language appeal to sight, sound, smell, or touch?
- **Emotional Impressions:** What feelings does the poem evoke? Is it joyful, mournful, angry?
- **Moral Impressions:** Does the poem teach a lesson or promote virtues? Does it inspire or repel?

**Why focus on impressions?** They connect the reader personally to the poem, transforming abstract words into visceral experiences. For example, vivid imagery might evoke a sense of nostalgia or longing.

## 4. Marking Meter

How Meter, is like the invisible border of the poem. 

Meter is described as **the invisible border of a poem**, discovered through the cadence of stressed and unstressed syllables. Key points include:

- **Identification:** Recognizing common meters like iambic pentameter.
- **Function:** Understanding how meter influences tone, pace, and mood.
- **Variation:** Not all poets use the same meter; effects differ accordingly.

**Tools provided:** A one-sheet guide on metric feet and line length helps in quick identification. For example, Shakespeare’s use of iambic pentameter lends a natural, conversational rhythm, while other meters might create a more formal or musical effect.

## 5. Surveying Structure

Forms, how does the form of a poem inform us as a reader that here are many kinds of structure, like visual, audible, internal rhymes, logical structure, Volta, emotional structure.

This step examines **the form and organization** of the poem, including:

- **Visual Structure:** Line length, stanza arrangement, spacing.
- **Audible Structure:** Rhyme schemes, internal rhymes, sound patterns.
- **Logical Structure:** The poem’s turn or *volta*, shifts in argument or emotion.
- **Emotional Structure:** The progression of feelings, guiding the reader from one emotional state to another.

**Significance:** Structure shapes meaning and emotional impact. For example, a sonnet’s volta often signals a shift in tone or argument, crucial for interpretation.
