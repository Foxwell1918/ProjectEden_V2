# Progress

### **Measuring Progress**

Sanderson stresses that **writers control progress**—the *how* and *when* of story advancement. **Progress** can be **external** (e.g., characters reaching a destination) or **internal** (e.g., character growth). Effective storytelling **signposts progress** through:

- **Chapter endings** that hint at upcoming developments.
- **Obstacles** that escalate tension.
- **Information reveals** that move the plot forward.

He warns against **stagnation**—when the story **fails to deliver on promises**, leading to reader boredom or frustration.

### **Pacing and Signposting**

The **pace** should **match the story’s tone and structure**. For fast-paced thrillers, **short chapters with hooks** work well; for slower, character-driven stories, **breathers and reflection** are necessary. Sanderson advises **varying pacing** to **maintain reader engagement**.

### **Handling Travel and Time Jumps**

To **speed up travel scenes**, Sanderson recommends **early promises** of **time jumps** (e.g., “and then they arrived”). **Chapter breaks** or **signaling phrases** like “three weeks later” help **manage pacing** without confusing readers.

### **Twists and Surprises**

**Effective twists** rely on **signposting**—**foreshadowing** and **misdirection**. Sanderson compares twists to **gifts**—**surprising yet inevitable**. He illustrates this with examples like ***While You Were Sleeping***, where **escalation** (e.g., a lie revealed) **creates emotional payoff**.

He emphasizes **escalation**—**building obstacles**—and **red herrings**—**misleading clues**—to **maximize surprise**. The **ultimate goal** is **satisfying resolution**, where **payoff** feels **both surprising and earned**.
